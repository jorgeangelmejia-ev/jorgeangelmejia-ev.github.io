The Longevity Market Overlooked

By Jorge Ángel Mejía · 2026-11-10

A neglected dimension of longevity

Longevity has often been presented through performance metrics and biohacking imagery. But improving the quality of years lived requires a more inclusive approach to health.

The World Economic Forum and McKinsey Health Institute reported that women spend approximately 25% more of their lives in poor health than men, despite longer average lifespans. Their model suggests that closing the women’s health gap could add at least $1 trillion annually to the global economy by 2040.

That $1 trillion is a modeled societal benefit—not forecast revenue for spas, hotels or residential developers.

What a more relevant wellness product looks like

Women’s health needs vary across life stages and include preventive care, cardiovascular health, sleep, pregnancy and postpartum recovery, perimenopause, menopause and healthy aging.

For hospitality and residential real estate, responsible differentiation could include accessible movement, privacy, restorative architecture, education and partnerships with qualified medical professionals.

None of these features replaces individualized medical care, and developers should avoid making clinical claims for unvalidated therapies.

The Riviera Maya opportunity

International second-home buyers may value service coordination, language accessibility, reliable referral networks and continuity between their primary healthcare providers and their time in Mexico.

In the Riviera Maya, a genuine female-wellness proposition would depend on licensed providers, transparent service availability and practical access to medical facilities—not simply a branded spa suite.

In Tulum, developers should be especially careful to distinguish between a restorative lifestyle experience and healthcare services that require professional infrastructure.

Underwriting a women-centered offering

• Confirm which services are actually available on site and which require referral.

  • Assess operator qualifications, insurance, privacy practices and applicable regulation.
  • Model utilization by resident type and season rather than assuming universal demand.
  • Account for recurring staffing and specialized equipment costs.
  • Test whether the offering improves buyer preference, occupancy or retention using observable data.

The most defensible premium may come from trust, reliability and service continuity—not from the number of treatment rooms.

Closing perspective

The evolution of female wellness is an opportunity to replace generic luxury language with a more accurate understanding of residents’ needs.

The winning development may not be the one promising to reverse aging. It may be the one that designs thoughtfully, partners responsibly and respects the complexity of real health.
